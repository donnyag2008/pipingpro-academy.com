Pipe Properties

In the long history of human civilisation, the design of piping networks has been practised for thousands of years — bamboo irrigation pipes were used in China as early as 3000–2000 BC. Rapid development came in the 19th century, with a major advance in pipe stress theory in 1934 when R. H. Tingey published "Method of Calculating Thermal Expansion Stresses in Piping".

Today, a piping system is pipe sections joined with fittings, valves, and equipment, supported by hangers and supports. Pipe is just one element of the complete system. This module covers the fundamental properties of pipe and the materials used in Oil & Gas.

Lesson 1.1Pipe Material Classification

1.1.1  The Two Main Categories

Materials widely used for pipes and their components fall into two main categories:

  • Metallic — divided into Ferrous and Non-Ferrous. Ferrous groups into cast iron and steel; non-ferrous covers aluminium, nickel, copper, etc.
  • Non-metallic — Plastic pipes (thermoplastic and thermosetting) and non-plastic pipes (typically concrete and glass).
From the field

When I started my career in 1990, joining an engineering firm in Jakarta on a Middle East oil refinery project, I was thrown in at the deep end with very limited knowledge of piping materials. There was no book like this to help me. Carbon steel was the dominant material, but knowing when to use stainless, alloy, or non-metallic pipe is a skill that took years to develop. This module gives you that foundation from day one.

1.1.2  Ferrous Metallic Pipe — Cast Iron

Cast iron is an alloy of iron and carbon with carbon content above 2 wt%, often combined with silicon, nickel, or chromium to improve corrosion resistance. It is strong in compression but low in ductility and malleability, and very brittle — unsuitable for pressure piping. The most common types are white and grey cast iron; magnesium and cerium are added to increase ductility.

  • Application: sewer systems — not Oil & Gas process piping.
  • Key specifications: ASTM A-74 (cast iron soil pipe), ASTM A-888 (hubless cast iron), ANSI B16.45 (cast iron fittings).

1.1.3  Ferrous Metallic Pipe — Ductile Iron

Ductile iron is stronger and tougher than cast iron — a high-carbon magnesium-treated product containing graphite as spheroids, giving significantly better impact resistance and ductility than grey cast iron.

  • Application: sewage service and potable water distribution — not used for process hydrocarbon piping.
  • Key specifications: ASTM A716, ASTM A746, AWWA C150.

1.1.4  Carbon Steel Pipe — The Workhorse of Oil & Gas

Carbon steel is the most popular and widely used pipe material in Oil & Gas — wide availability, high strength, and a huge range of connection systems and fittings. Steel is an alloy of iron with no more than 2 wt% carbon.

By the amount of oxygen removed during steelmaking (deoxidising), steel is categorised as Killed Steel (fully deoxidised), Semi-Killed Steel (partially deoxidised), and Rimmed Steel (no deoxidising agent, carbon 0.12–0.15%).

Three general groups by carbon content:

  • Low Carbon Steel: 0.05–0.25% carbon
  • Medium Carbon Steel: 0.25–0.50% carbon
  • High Carbon Steel: 0.50% and greater

1.1.6  Stainless Steel Pipe

Stainless steel is used wherever corrosion resistance is needed. A minimum of 10.5% chromium is required for a material to be called stainless. Three types:

  • Austenitic (300 series — 304, 316, 316L): max 0.15% C, min 16% Cr, plus Ni and/or Mn. Excellent strength, ductility, and corrosion resistance. Non-magnetic. The most common stainless in O&G piping.
  • Ferritic (400 series): Cr 14–27%. Magnetic.
  • Martensitic: Cr 11.5–18%. Magnetic. Stronger than austenitic but lower corrosion resistance.

Low-carbon austenitic grades carry the "L" suffix (e.g. 316L). Lower carbon prevents sensitisation during welding (chromium-carbide precipitation at grain boundaries), eliminating the need for PWHT in most cases.

  • Application: food, beverage, pharmaceutical, offshore, chloride-rich environments. Never installed underground. Generally no external coating needed.
  • Key specifications: ASTM A312 (seamless/welded austenitic), ASTM A790 (ferritic/austenitic duplex).

Lesson 1.2Pipe Sizes, Schedules & Key Formulas

1.2.1  NPS and DN — The Size Designation Systems

A pipe is a tube with a round cross-section having an outside diameter (OD), a wall thickness, and a resulting inside diameter (ID). Pipe sizes conform to ASME B36.10M-2022 (welded & seamless wrought steel) and ASME B36.19M-2022 (stainless steel).

  • NPS (Nominal Pipe Size): a dimensionless designator. For NPS 14 and above, NPS = OD in inches. For NPS 12 and below, OD is always larger than the NPS number.
  • DN (Diamètre Nominal): the metric equivalent — approximate ID in millimetres (e.g. DN 50 ≈ 50 mm ID).

Pipes are grouped as: Large Bore (> 2 in), Small Bore (≤ 2 in), and Tubing (up to 4 in, thinner walls than standard pipe).

Note

NPS differs from OD below NPS 14 because in the 1930s pipes were made on inner diameter with a 1/16-inch wall, so the OD became larger by 1/8 inch. This legacy is why NPS 6 has an OD of 6.625 in, not 6.000 in.

1.2.2  Schedule Numbers

Wall thickness once had only three designations: Standard (STD), Extra Strong (XS), and Double Extra Strong (XXS). Schedule numbers were later added: 5S, 10S, 10, 20, 30, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120, 140, 160. The suffix "S" indicates availability in stainless steel.

Note

Schedule 40 = STD for NPS 1/8 through NPS 10. For NPS 12 and above, STD = 9.53 mm wall regardless of schedule. Schedule 80 = XS for NPS 8 and below; all larger XS = 12.70 mm (½ in) wall.

Lesson 1.3Mechanical Properties

1.3.1  Modulus of Elasticity (Young's Modulus)

The Modulus of Elasticity (E) is the ratio of normal stress to corresponding strain in the elastic region (Hooke's Law) — a measure of material stiffness. E varies with temperature: the higher the temperature, the softer the material and the lower its E. This matters for stress analysis:

  • For high-temperature piping — use E at the cold (ambient) temperature for sustained and expansion stress calculations, per ASME B31.3.
  • For low/cold lines — use E at the minimum temperature.

1.3.2  Yield Strength & Ultimate Tensile Strength

Strength is the ability to resist deformation or failure under applied force. Two key values:

  • Yield Strength (SMYS): the maximum stress maintaining elastic behaviour before plastic deformation begins. Determined by the 0.2% offset method.
  • Ultimate Tensile Strength (SMTS): the maximum applied load ÷ original cross-sectional area sustained before breaking — the highest point on the stress-strain curve.

Lesson 1.5Post Weld Heat Treatment (PWHT)

1.5.1  Purpose of PWHT

PWHT is applied to a weld to improve mechanical properties, corrosion resistance, microstructure, and environmental cracking resistance, and to reduce residual stress. Residual welding stresses can cause stress corrosion cracking (SCC), hydrogen-induced cracking (HIC), and premature fatigue failure — particularly in sour service.

1.5.2  When PWHT Is Required

The requirement depends on code, material, wall thickness, and carbon content:

  • ASME B31.4 & B31.8 (2025): PWHT for carbon steel above certain wall thickness limits — e.g. > 1.25 in (32 mm) in some cases. Depends on carbon content, carbon equivalent, and wall thickness.
  • ASME B31.3 & B31.1: PWHT on shop/field girth welds based on material P-group, wall thickness, carbon content, and UTS. E.g. P-1 carbon steel requires PWHT above 19 mm (¾ in) nominal wall for most applications.
  • Sour service (NACE MR0175): PWHT mandatory for all carbon steel welds in H₂S service to reduce HAZ hardness below HRC 22.
  • Chrome-moly (P11, P22, P91): PWHT mandatory for all welds — no exceptions. Without it, brittle martensite forms in the HAZ and the risk of hydrogen cracking rises sharply.
Important

PWHT requirements vary by code edition, material group, wall thickness, and carbon equivalent. Always check the project-specific code edition — do not rely on memory or previous project experience. The consequence of missing a PWHT requirement is a weld that can fail in service.

Key point

PWHT is not optional when required by code. It is a controlled, documented process performed by qualified welders and witnessed or verified by inspection. On Aramco projects, PWHT is a mandatory hold point in the Inspection and Test Plan (ITP).

Lesson 2.2Stainless Steel: Austenitic, Duplex & Special Grades

2.2.1  Why Stainless Steel?

Stainless steel is used wherever corrosion resistance is needed. A minimum of 10.5% chromium forms a passive chromium-oxide layer that resists rust and staining — and this layer self-heals when damaged in the presence of oxygen.

2.2.2  Austenitic Stainless Steel — 300 Series

Identified by the 300 series: max 0.15% C, min 16% Cr, plus Ni and/or Mn. Excellent strength, ductility, and corrosion resistance; non-magnetic when annealed.

Important

Always specify 316L (not 316) for welded stainless piping unless you intend to perform post-weld solution annealing. Using standard 316 for welded pipe without heat treatment is a common specification error that leads to intergranular corrosion in service.

2.2.3  Ferritic & Martensitic Stainless Steel

  • Ferritic (400 series): Cr 14–27%, magnetic. Lower cost than austenitic but limited corrosion resistance and lower sub-zero toughness.
  • Martensitic: Cr 11.5–18%, magnetic. Higher strength than austenitic but much lower corrosion resistance. Used for valve trim, shafts, wear parts — not pipe.

2.2.4  Duplex Stainless Steel

Duplex has a mixed microstructure of ~50% austenite and 50% ferrite. This two-phase structure gives much higher strength than standard austenitic grades and excellent SCC and chloride-pitting resistance — the material of choice for aggressive offshore and sour environments.

Lesson 2.3Alloy Steel: Chrome-Moly for High Temperature

2.3.1  When Alloy Steel Is Needed

When process temperatures exceed 427°C — the upper limit for carbon steel — you must step up to alloy steels. The primary additions are Chromium and Molybdenum, improving creep resistance, oxidation resistance, and elevated-temperature strength. These chrome-moly (Cr-Mo) steels are specified under ASTM A335 for seamless pipe.

Creep is the slow, time-dependent plastic deformation under sustained stress at elevated temperature. It causes permanent deformation, loss of bolt preload at flanges, and ultimately failure. Chrome-moly steels resist creep where carbon steel would deform unacceptably.

2.3.2  Preheat & PWHT — Mandatory for All P-Grade Steels

Chrome-moly steels are much harder to weld than carbon steel. Higher alloy content increases hardenability — the tendency to form hard, brittle martensite in the HAZ. Two heat-treatment steps are mandatory:

  1. Preheat — the pipe is heated before welding to slow cooling and prevent martensite. Minimums: P11 ≈ 150°C; P22 ≈ 200°C; P91 ≈ 200–250°C.
  2. PWHT — after welding, the joint is held at tempering temperature to relieve residual stress and temper the HAZ. Temperatures: P11 = 677–746°C; P22 = 677–760°C; P91 = 730–800°C.
Important

P91 is notoriously difficult to weld. Incorrect PWHT — insufficient hold temperature or time — produces Type IV cracking, which has caused multiple catastrophic failures globally. Always ensure WPS/PQR and PWHT records are fully controlled and verified by a qualified welding engineer.

Key point

Above 427°C: specify P11 or P22. Above 566°C: P91. Preheat and PWHT are mandatory — no exceptions for any chrome-moly weld. This is both a code requirement and a safety requirement.

Lesson 2.4Non-Ferrous Metallic Pipes

2.4.1  Aluminium Alloy Pipe

Aluminium alloys are ~1/3 the density of steel, malleable, readily weldable, and corrosion-resistant. Uncommon in O&G piping, but found in compressed gas and cryogenic service.

  • Key standards: ASTM B210 (drawn seamless tube), ASTM B241 (seamless pipe and extruded tube).
  • Not widely used in O&G process piping due to cost and limited pressure/temperature capability vs steel.

2.4.2  Copper Tubing

Copper, bronze, and brass have long been used for piping due to excellent corrosion resistance, particularly for water. Copper tubing is most common in plumbing, hydronics, and refrigeration.

  • Key standards: ANSI B16.15 (cast copper alloy threaded fittings), ANSI B16.22 (wrought copper solder-joint pressure fittings).
  • In O&G, primarily instrument air, utility water, and HVAC within plant buildings — not process hydrocarbon piping.

Lesson 2.5Non-Metallic Pipe: Plastic Pipes

2.5.1  Growing Importance in Oil & Gas

Non-metallic pipes attract increasing attention for low maintenance, easy installation, and high corrosion resistance — especially in sour or seawater service. Two forms: Thermoplastics (repeatedly softened/hardened by heating/cooling without affecting properties) and Thermosetting (irreversibly hardened during manufacture — cannot be reshaped once cured).

2.5.2  High Density Polyethylene (HDPE)

HDPE is a thermoplastic (polyolefin family), flexible but tough, with excellent chemical resistance. Increasingly accepted for produced water, seawater injection, and low-pressure gas.

Pipeline material with internal cladding is increasingly replacing carbon steel with high corrosion allowance. Lesson 2.7Material Selection for Service Conditions

2.7.1  The Material Selection Process

Material selection follows a systematic process based on fluid composition, operating temperature, operating pressure, and environment. The piping engineer works with the process engineer (who provides the process data sheet) and the materials/corrosion engineer for the final selection. Key questions for any line:

  1. What is the fluid? — hydrocarbon, water, acid, steam, gas, chemical?
  2. What is the design temperature? — below −29°C? above 427°C?
  3. What is the design pressure? — determines pressure class and minimum wall.
  4. Is there H₂S present? — sour service triggers NACE MR0175.
  5. Is there CO₂ present? — carbonic acid corrosion in water-wet systems.
  6. What is the chloride content? — drives SS grade and SCC risk.
  7. What is the flow velocity? — high velocity can cause erosion, especially with sand or solids.

2.7.2  Sour Service — NACE MR0175 / ISO 15156

Sour service is any system containing H₂S at or above the NACE MR0175 threshold. H₂S causes Sulphide Stress Cracking (SSC) — hydrogen embrittlement where H₂S promotes hydrogen diffusion into the steel lattice, causing cracking under tensile stress. NACE MR0175 / ISO 15156 defines:

  • Maximum hardness limits for carbon and low-alloy steels (HRC 22 max for most applications).
  • Restrictions on high-strength steels above certain yield limits.
  • PWHT requirements on carbon steel welds in sour service.
  • Specific qualification requirements for stainless steels and nickel alloys.
Important

On any project with H₂S in the process fluid — even trace amounts above the NACE threshold — sour service requirements apply to ALL wetted components: pipe, fittings, valves, flanges. This must be captured in your piping material class from day one.

Lesson 3.3Allowable Pipe Span

3.3.1  Why Span Matters

The maximum allowable distance between supports — the Allowable Pipe Span — is critical for:

  • Protecting against excessive stress (overstress) — keeping bending stress within material limits.
  • Protecting against excessive deflection — avoiding pockets in liquid lines and interference with other pipes.
  • Controlling natural frequency — avoiding vibration resonance.

3.3.2  The Two Span Formulas

Two methods determine the maximum span — one based on bending stress, one on deflection. The controlling (smaller) value governs.

Formula 1 — Maximum Bending Stress
σ = 1.2 × (w × L²) / Z
σ in psi
Formula 2 — Maximum Deflection
δ = 17.1 × (w × L⁴) / (E × I)
δ in inches · w = unit weight (lb/ft): pipe + fluid + insulation · L = span (ft) · E = Modulus (psi) · I = moment of inertia (in⁴) · Z = section modulus (in³)

Source: Design of Piping Systems, The M.W. Kellogg Company, Revised 2nd Ed., 1965.

For power piping, some designers limit deflection to as little as 1/8 in. The practical limit for average process piping is ½ to 1 in. The main reason to limit deflection is to make the pipe stiff enough — high enough natural frequency — to avoid large-amplitude response under slight forces.

  • General process piping: a natural frequency of 4 Hz is reasonably satisfactory.
  • Lines on compressors / reciprocating equipment: 8 Hz or higher may be desirable, depending on the compressor.

3.3.3  Span Correction Factors for Special Configurations

The span formula applies to a straight run with supports at both ends. For other configurations — corners before elbows, expansion loops — multiply the calculated span L by a correction factor:

ConfigurationCorrection Factor
Straight pipe continuous runL × 1.0
At corner before elbow (direction change)L × 0.8
At expansion loop — leg perpendicular to main runL × 0.75
At expansion loop — leg parallel to main run (cantilever)L × 0.65
Note

These factors reflect reduced effective span stiffness when the pipe changes direction. An elbow or loop adds flexibility at the corner, reducing the effective support of the pipe at that location.

★Worked Example — NPS 4, SCH 40 (from the book)

Given: NPS 4, Schedule 40 carbon steel pipe, full of water. Maximum deflection allowed: 1 in.

Step 1 — Pipe Properties
OD = 4.500 in · t = 0.237 in ID = 4.500 − (2 × 0.237) = 4.026 in
Step 2 — Weights
wp = 10.6802 × (4.500 − 0.237) × 0.237 = 10.80 lb/ft ww = 0.3405 × 4.026² = 5.52 lb/ft w = 10.80 + 5.52 = 16.32 lb/ft
Step 3 — Moment of Inertia
I = 0.0491 × (4.500⁴ − 4.026⁴) = 7.23 in⁴
Step 4 — Modulus of Elasticity
Carbon Steel (C < 0.30%) @ 200°F: E = 28.8 × 10⁶ psi (ASME B31.3-2024 App. C)
Step 5 — Maximum Span (deflection limit 1 in)
L⁴ = (δ × E × I) / (17.1 × w) L⁴ = (1 × 28.8e6 × 7.23) / (17.1 × 16.32) L = 29.40 ft = 8.97 m

For a corner before an elbow, apply the 0.8 factor: 29.40 × 0.8 = 23.52 ft (7.17 m).

From the field

In practice, most companies maintain standard span tables derived from this formula for common sizes and services. You rarely calculate from first principles on every line — instead you reference the project span table and calculate specifically only for unusual cases such as heavy valves, large-diameter lines, or non-standard fluid densities.

Lesson 3.4Standard Pipe Support Types

3.4.1  Welded Shoe

A welded shoe sits between the bottom of the pipe and the support structure, carrying the load and weight of the pipe and contents. Two types: for uninsulated and insulated lines. Shoes are generally the same material as the pipe (carbon steel). For non-CS pipe (e.g. SS), the shoe is still typically CS but must not be welded directly — it is clamped instead. If welding directly would overstress the wall (large-bore thin-wall pipes), a Reinforcing Pad is used between shoe and pipe wall.

3.4.2  Clamped Shoe

Used where no welding to the main pipe is allowed — different material or insulation. The standard choice for:

  • Stainless steel pipes — CS shoe welded directly to SS is unacceptable (dissimilar metals).
  • Alloy steel pipes (P11, P22, P91) — shoe material differs from pipe material.
  • Any case where PWHT would be disrupted by the shoe weld.
Note

Although primarily associated with non-CS pipe, the clamped shoe is also widely used on carbon steel on many EPC projects as a standard approach to avoid the risk of weld defects on the pipe body.

3.4.3  Cold Shoe (Cryogenic & Cold Service)

Cold shoes serve lines with fluid temperatures between −195°C and +82°C — cryogenic or cold insulation service. They differ fundamentally from hot shoes by including Polyurethane Foam (PUF) inside the shoe body, acting as both a load-retention element and a thermal break. A cold shoe assembly typically has:

  • Metal protection jacket (usually aluminium) — outer protective layer
  • PUF insert — thermal insulation and load distribution
  • Vapour barrier — prevents moisture ingress and condensation on the cold surface
Important

Cold shoes must be manufactured by a specialist with cryogenic experience. PUF material selection and jacket design are critical — incorrect cold shoe design causes ice formation on support steel, cold bridging to structure, and potential structural damage.

3.4.4  Guide

A guide prevents lateral movement while allowing axial (along-pipe) thermal expansion. Usually structural steel cut to size; used for bare or insulated pipe. Placement on critical lines is determined by the stress engineer; for rack lines the piping designer places guides per normal practice. Like all standard supports, guides have an allowable load limit per designation.

3.4.5  Line Stop (Limit Stop)

A Line Stop (Axial Stop) prevents or limits axial movement. Because it must withstand axial thermal force, it must be welded to the pipe (uninsulated) or to the shoe (insulated). If the force exceeds the standard line stop allowable, a Special Support must be designed — typically handled by the Civil/Structural group.

3.4.6  Anchor

The Anchor (Full Fixation) allows no movement in any direction — vertical, lateral, axial, or rotational. Placed at strategic locations:

  • Mid-way between two expansion loops — to control expansion direction to each loop.
  • Near equipment connections — to limit forces and moments reaching the nozzle.
  • At pipe rack tiers — to divide the rack into independent expansion segments.
From the field

Near equipment, be very careful with rigid welded anchors. In my experience it's often better to use a combination of guide + line stop + shoe (and a hold-up support if needed) rather than a single rigid welded anchor. This is more easily adjustable and causes less stress concentration at the support attachment weld. Many EPC companies prefer this combination method — full fixation function without the rigidity risk.

3.4.7  Adjustable Support

Often used near pump nozzles (or elsewhere per the stress engineer), its height can be adjusted to accommodate elevation changes — e.g. when a pump nozzle moves during installation or settlement occurs. It consists of a trunnion (short pipe stub) welded to the pipe, a base plate, and adjustable nuts and bolts. Particularly valuable near rotating equipment for fine elevation control to match nozzle alignment.

3.4.8  Reinforcing Pad

Additional wall thickness added externally to provide structural integrity and prevent buckling — particularly for large-bore thin-wall pipes. Used either as a standalone load-bearing attachment point, or in combination with a shoe or trunnion to strengthen the pipe wall against vertical and lateral loads.

3.4.9  Trunnion Support

A trunnion uses pipe material as the support member itself. Used at elbows, on vertical runs, and on horizontal runs for large or heavy lines. It transfers load to the structure via the short pipe stub rather than through the pipe wall directly.

Rule of Thumb — Trunnion Size (from the book)
Trunnion Size = (Main Pipe NPS ÷ 2) ↑ next standard size
Example: Main pipe NPS 10 → 10 ÷ 2 = 5 → round up to NPS 6. Minimum trunnion size = NPS 6.

For loads within the catalogue allowable, select a standard trunnion. For loads exceeding it, perform a formal trunnion strength calculation (method shown in the book's Exhibits 4 and 5).

3.4.10  Spring Hangers — Variable & Constant

Spring hangers (Resilient Supports) use helical springs to support pipe while allowing vertical movement under thermal expansion. Two types:

Variable Spring Hanger

A coil spring that compresses/extends as the pipe moves. Support load varies with deflection (F = k·x). Used where vertical movement is small and load variation acceptable.

Variability Factor
VF = (Travel × Spring Rate) / Operating Load × 100%
equivalently: VF = (Operating Load − Installation Load) / Operating Load × 100%
  • General standard: Variability Factor ≤ 25%
  • Pipes connected to rotating equipment: Variability Factor ≤ 12%

If VF exceeds 25%, use a Constant Spring Support instead.

Constant Effort (Constant Load) Spring Hanger

A spring operated through a lever mechanism so that as load and displacement increase, the lever compensates — maintaining an approximately constant force regardless of vertical position. Used for:

  • Hot lines with large vertical thermal movement (where variable VF would exceed 25%).
  • Pipes on sensitive rotating equipment where support load must not change between cold and hot.
  • Very heavy pipes with large movements requiring consistent support load.
From the field

When selecting spring hangers, always ensure enough vertical space exists — above or below the pipe — to fit not only the spring but also any rod, shoe, trunnion, or other components. I've seen spring selections that were technically correct but couldn't be physically installed because the space calculation wasn't done. Check the space first.

Note

Spring variability ≤ 25% general; ≤ 12% for rotating equipment. These are industry-standard practice, not a code requirement — but violating them causes unacceptable load shifts between cold and hot conditions that can damage equipment nozzles.

Lesson 3.5Special Pipe Supports

3.5.1  When Standard Supports Are Not Sufficient

Special supports are designed where standard supports cannot be used, because:

  • The imposed force exceeds the allowable for a standard support at the given pipe size.
  • The pipe location makes a standard support impossible — e.g. far from existing structural steel.

Special supports may use standard supports as a design basis, modified to suit. Their design is not regulated directly by ASME B31.1 or B31.3 — engineers use experience, MSS SP-58, and structural principles. For complex special supports, the work is typically handed to the Civil/Structural group, who design using structural codes (AISC, BS 5950, or equivalent).

Key point

Always try a standard support first. Special supports take more engineering time, more approval steps, and more fabrication cost. Use them only when you have genuinely exhausted all standard options.

Codes & Standards

"Codes and standards" is a phrase every piping engineer must know well. A standard is a technical definition or directive addressed to designers, manufacturers, operators, or users of equipment. A code is a standard that has been adopted by a regulatory authority and given legal force.

Together, they establish the minimum requirements for safe construction. Their primary purpose is to protect public safety — defining rules for materials, design, fabrication, examination, inspection, and testing. Failure to follow these rules increases the risk of accidents.

Lesson 4.1What Are Codes and Standards?

4.1.1  The Distinction Between a Code and a Standard

Engineers often use "code" and "standard" interchangeably — but there is an important technical distinction:

TermDefinitionExample
StandardA technical document defining requirements for materials, design, fabrication, testing, or operation. Published by a standards organisation. Compliance voluntary unless mandated by law or contract.ASME B36.10 — Welded and Seamless Wrought Steel Pipe (defines pipe dimensions)
CodeA standard adopted by a regulatory authority and given legal force. Compliance mandatory where adopted.ASME B31.3 — Process Piping Code (mandated by law in most jurisdictions for process plant piping)

On any O&G project, both codes and standards apply — some because they are legally required, others because the contract or client specification mandates them.

4.1.2  What Codes and Standards Do — and Do NOT Do

Perfect materials and flawless fabrication are rare. Experience shows that meeting the code's minimum requirements and safety factors dramatically reduces the likelihood of failure and protects lives. Codes use simplified methods for common problems; experienced designers may use more advanced analysis, but any alternative method must still satisfy the code's requirements for design, construction, inspection, examination, and testing.

From the field

From my own experience: it is not easy to convince a client that a method different from what the Code prescribes is safe — even if your engineering justification is sound. The Code carries legal authority. If you deviate, you need strong validation and client written approval. Always document it.

Important

Codes and Standards set the minimum floor for safety. They do not tell you how to achieve the best or most efficient design — they tell you the minimum that is acceptable. Good engineers design above the minimum, using judgment, experience, and analysis to find the best solution within the code framework.

Lesson 4.2Categories of Standards Organisations

As noted by George Antaki in Piping and Pipeline Engineering, standards divide into four categories. Knowing which category an organisation belongs to helps you understand the type of standards it produces.

4.2.1  Professional Societies

Publish standards on design, construction, and maintenance for their profession; governments may adopt them as codes. Driven by technical experts and academia in the public interest.

ASME · ASCE · AIChE · AISC · ACI

4.2.2  Trade Associations

Associations of equipment or product manufacturers — standards explain how to use members' products safely and effectively. Highly practical and product-specific.

API · AWWA · AISI · NIDI

4.2.3  Testing and Certification Organisations

Certify that products meet defined safety requirements. They do not typically write design codes, but their certification marks tell you equipment has been independently tested.

UL · FM · ICBO Evaluation Services

4.2.4  Standard Developing Organisations

Create consensus standards improving reliability, safety, and interoperability. ASTM International is the most important example for piping engineers — it develops the material specifications for virtually every pipe, fitting, and component you will specify.

Key point

When you see ASTM on a pipe specification (e.g. ASTM A106 Gr. B), you are referencing an ASTM material standard. When you see ASME on a design document (e.g. ASME B31.3), you are referencing a design code. Both are essential and both appear constantly in daily work.

Lesson 4.7ASME B31.3 Fluid Service Categories

4.7.1  Why Fluid Service Categories Matter

ASME B31.3 does not treat all systems the same. It categorises piping by the combination of fluid properties, operating conditions, and risk profile. The category directly determines the level of examination (NDE), the joint types permitted, fabrication quality, leak test requirements, and the design/inspection philosophy. You will find the fluid service category on the Line Designation Table (LDT) or piping material class index for every line.

4.7.2  The Fluid Service Categories

Normal Fluid Service

The most common category — the vast majority of process piping. Not subject to the special rules of Category D, M, Elevated Temperature, High Pressure, or High Purity. Standard B31.3 requirements apply.

Category D Fluid Service

A less demanding category for low-risk utility and non-hazardous lines. ALL of the following must apply:

  • Fluid is non-flammable, non-toxic, and not damaging to human tissues
  • Design gauge pressure ≤ 150 psi (1,035 kPa)
  • Design temperature ≤ 186°C (366°F)
  • Fluid temperature (other than from atmospheric conditions) not less than −29°C (−20°F)

Category D allows simplified examination — threaded and socket weld joints may be used more freely, and examination requirements are less stringent than Normal.

Note

Typical Category D lines in a gas plant: instrument air, plant air, low-temperature cooling water returns, low-pressure inert gas. Note the word ALL — if any one condition is not met, the line cannot be Category D.

Category M Fluid Service

The most demanding category — highly hazardous fluids where a single exposure to a very small quantity can cause serious irreversible harm even with prompt medical response. Both must apply:

  • The fluid is so highly toxic that a single small-quantity leak exposure can produce serious irreversible harm on breathing or bodily contact, even with prompt restorative measures
  • The owner determines, after considering design, experience, service conditions, and location, that Normal Fluid Service requirements do not sufficiently provide the leak-tightness needed to protect personnel

Category M carries the most stringent examination and leak-testing requirements. Design rules are in Chapter VIII of B31.3.

From the field

It is the owner's responsibility to determine if a fluid service is Category M — not the EPC contractor. ASME B31.3 Appendix M provides a logic flowchart for the owner. On Aramco projects, the category is typically defined in the Process Hazards Analysis (PHA) and confirmed in the Line Designation Table.

Elevated Temperature Fluid Service

Applies when the metal temperature is sustained at or above the creep threshold (Tcr) in B31.3 Table 302.3.5. Additional design requirements apply to prevent creep failure over the design life.

Note

For carbon steel, Tcr ≈ 371°C (700°F). For chrome-moly steels (P11, P22, P91), the creep regime starts at higher temperatures. Always check Table 302.3.5 for the specific material.

High Pressure Fluid Service

Applies when the owner specifies Chapter IX of B31.3 for piping exceeding the pressure ratings of ASME B16.5 Class 2500 — the most stringent design, fabrication, and examination requirements of all normal categories.

High Purity Fluid Service

For systems requiring alternative methods of fabrication, inspection, examination, and testing to produce a controlled level of cleanliness — high purity, ultra-high purity, hygienic, or aseptic systems. Most common in pharma, semiconductor, and food processing rather than O&G.

Lesson 5.1Pipe Sizing, Formulas & Worked Examples

5.1.1  Pipe Size Designation — NPS and DN

A pipe is a tube with a round cross-section — an OD, a wall thickness, and a resulting ID. Pipe size, wall thickness, weights, and masses must conform to ASME B36.10-2022 (Welded and Seamless Wrought Steel Pipe) and ASME B36.19-2022 (Stainless Steel Pipe).

  • NPS (Nominal Pipe Size): a dimensionless size designator — e.g. NPS 1½, NPS 12.
  • DN (Diamètre Nominal): the metric equivalent — approximate ID in mm (DN 50 ≈ 50 mm).

Grouped as Large Bore (> 2 in), Small Bore (≤ 2 in), and Tubing (up to 4 in, thinner walls).

Note

For NPS 14 and larger, OD = NPS in inches. For NPS 12 and below, OD is larger than the nominal size — a legacy of 1930s manufacturing where pipes were made on inner diameter with a 1/16-inch wall, making the OD larger by 1/8 inch.

5.1.2  Schedule Numbers and Wall Thickness

Originally only STD, XS, and XXS. As stronger, corrosion-resistant materials enabled thinner pipes, the Schedule system was added for precise ordering: 5/5S, 10/10S, then 20, 30, 40, then 60, 80, 100, 120, 140, 160. The "S" suffix indicates stainless availability.

Note

Schedule 40 = STD for NPS ⅛ to 10 inclusive. Schedule 80 = XS for pipes up to NPS 8 inclusive. All larger XS = ½ in (12.70 mm) wall.

Lesson 5.2Pipe Fittings

5.2.1  What Are Pipe Fittings?

A piping system requires components such as elbows, flanges, weldolets, threadolets, tees, reducers, and caps — collectively "fittings." They are made of the same material as the pipe and are governed primarily by ASME B16.9 (butt-welding fittings) and ASME B16.11 (socket-weld and threaded forged fittings).

5.2.2  Elbows

An elbow changes the direction of flow — 45° or 90°. Two types:

  • Short Radius (SR): bend radius R = 1.0 × D. Higher pressure drop and higher stress intensification — use with caution.
  • Long Radius (LR): bend radius R = 1.5 × D. Preferred for all standard applications — lower pressure drop, lower SIF, better flow.

A Reducing Elbow has different inlet/outlet diameters — used where a direction change and size reduction occur together.

From the field

In 35 years I have used long radius elbows as the default for virtually all applications. Short radius elbows are specified only where space is genuinely too restricted — and even then, the stress engineer must check the higher SIF carefully.

5.2.3  Caps

A cap closes the end of a pipe (same function as a plug) — permanently or temporarily, e.g. at future tie-in points or during pressure testing.

5.2.4  Reducers — Concentric and Eccentric

A reducer transitions between a larger and smaller diameter. Two types:

  • Concentric Reducer: upstream and downstream share the same centreline. Used anywhere only a size change is needed and centreline alignment isn't a concern.
  • Eccentric Reducer: offset centrelines, with a flat side. Two orientations below.
  • Flat-Bottom (BOE): flat at the bottom. Used in pipe racks so the pipe rests at the same support elevation — the most common orientation for horizontal rack pipe.
  • Flat-Top (TOE): flat at the top. The most common application is at pump suction nozzles, to prevent air pockets that could cause cavitation.
From the field

The Flat-Top Eccentric Reducer at pump suction is one of the most important details in piping design. Installing a concentric reducer or a Flat-Bottom at pump suction causes air entrainment and pump cavitation. Always check this detail personally on any pump suction line.

5.2.5  Tees

  • Equal (Straight) Tee: inlet and both outlets the same diameter — the most-used configuration.
  • Reducing Tee: branch outlet smaller than the run pipe. Used when branch flow is significantly less than main run flow.

5.2.6  Stub-In Connections

A stub-in branches by cutting a hole in the run pipe and welding the branch pipe directly — without a Tee. Cheaper and easier to install, popular for medium and lower pressure. May be installed with or without a reinforcing pad; whether a pad is required is determined by the branch reinforcement calculation (ASME B31.3 para 304.3).

5.2.7  Olets

An Olet branches at 90° or 45° from a straight pipe. The choice between Tee and Olet depends on the size relationship:

  • Branch drops only one pipe size from the run — use a Reducing Tee.
  • Branch drops two or more sizes — use an Olet.

General practice: if a normal Tee connection is available, prefer the Tee — especially for high pressure/temperature — because Tees are forged with better mechanical integrity than field-fabricated olet connections. Three types by end connection: Weldolet (butt-weld), Sockolet (socket-weld, small bore), Threadolet (threaded, instrument/utility, low pressure only).

Key point

Branch selection is a daily decision. The rule: one size drop = Reducing Tee; two or more drops = Olet; same size = Equal Tee. Always confirm with your piping material class — some clients prohibit stub-ins in certain services.

5.3.5  Flange Pressure-Temperature Ratings

Flanges are grouped into pressure Classes: 150, 300, 400, 600, 900, 1500, 2500. These are P-T ratings — the maximum allowable working pressure at a given temperature for a specific material group. ASME B16.5 provides the ratings. The temperature shown is the component temperature, equal to the contained fluid temperature.

Example — Carbon Steel flange (Group 1.1, e.g. ASTM A105), per ASME B16.5-2020 Table 2-1.1C:

  • Class 150 at 100°F (38°C) → 285 psig
  • Class 150 at 500°F (260°C) → 170 psig
  • Class 300 at 100°F (38°C) → 740 psig
Important

Pressure rating DECREASES as temperature INCREASES. Always check the P-T rating at your actual design temperature — not at ambient. One of the most common errors made by junior engineers. A Class 150 flange at 260°C has significantly less than 285 psig capacity.

5.3.6  Working Pressure Equation

ASME B16.5 Appendix A-2 gives the working pressure for materials at any temperature within the tables. For Class 300 and higher:

Working Pressure at Temperature T
pt = (C₁ × S₁ / 8750) × pr  ≤  pc
pt = rated working pressure at T · C₁ = 10 (S₁ in MPa → bar) or 1 (S₁ in psi → psi) · S₁ = allowable stress at T · pr = class index (300, 600, …) · pc = ceiling pressure at T per Table A-1
Key point

Flanges are pressure-temperature rated, not pressure-only rated. The Class number is not the design pressure in psi. A Class 300 flange gives 740 psig at 100°F but may give only ~490 psig at 600°F. Always look up the P-T table for your specific material and temperature.

Lesson 5.4Gaskets

5.4.1  Why Gaskets Are Critical

Gaskets and bolting ensure the integrity of the connection between two flanges and prevent leakage. Leak performance depends on the gasket stress in operation — the higher the gasket stress, the higher the leak-tightness. Flange faces are never perfectly smooth, and rust and imperfections develop in service; the gasket fills these imperfections when compressed by bolt load. When choosing a gasket consider: compatibility with the process fluid, operating temperature and pressure, compressibility, and suitability for the flange material.

5.4.2  Gasket Properties

An ideal gasket has compressibility (conforms to surface finish, fills imperfections), resilience (moves with dynamic pressure/temperature cycles, maintaining seating stress), and stability (no significant thickness change under cyclic load or sustained elevated temperature — creep resistance).

5.4.4  Insulating Gaskets

Insulating gaskets prevent galvanic corrosion where dissimilar metals connect (e.g. CS flange to SS flange) and provide electrical isolation where aboveground connects to underground piping for cathodic protection. A kit consists of the insulating gasket body, insulating sleeves around each stud, and insulating + steel washers under each nut.

Important

Insulating gaskets are generally considered unreliable — minimise their use to areas where absolutely necessary, and only when agreed with the engineering team and client. Incorrect installation can defeat the corrosion protection entirely.

Key point

Spiral wound (SS/graphite) is the default for RF process piping (Class 150–2500). RTJ metallic ring joints for high-pressure pipeline and wellhead connections. Soft gaskets for low-pressure utilities only.

Lesson 5.5Bolts and Nuts

5.5.1  The Role of Bolting

Bolts and nuts provide the clamping force that compresses the gasket and maintains the seal. Correct grade, size, and tightening procedure are essential — the wrong grade or incorrect torque is one of the most common causes of flange leaks.

5.5.2  Types of Bolts

  • Stud Bolts: threaded at both ends or full length — the most common for pressure piping; tightened from both ends, easier to remove if corroded.
  • Machine Bolts: integral head, threaded one end. More common for structural connections.

By strength: Low (min yield ≤ 30 ksi), Medium (30–105 ksi), High (≥ 105 ksi).

Lesson 5.6Thermal Insulation

5.6.1  Purpose of Thermal Insulation

Insulation maintains the fluid temperature for process requirements by reducing heat flow between surfaces — reducing heat loss on hot piping and heat gain on cold piping. Three common design criteria:

  • Controlling heat loss (hot) or heat gain (cold)
  • Personnel protection — preventing burns from hot surfaces (typically above 60°C surface temperature)
  • Fire protection — passive fire protection on structural members and supports

5.6.2  Design Considerations

Insulation thickness is normally based on the normal operating temperature of the contained fluid, with additional considerations:

  • Non-jacketed piping with heat tracing — consider only the heated fluid's normal operating temperature.
  • Jacketed piping — the heating fluid temperature governs thickness selection.
  • Vertical vessels may vary in thickness top to bottom per the operating temperature profile.
  • Heat exchangers — use the higher of inlet/outlet temperatures for each side.
Note

Insulation weight must be included in your pipe stress analysis — it adds to the distributed weight load, affecting both support spacing and the sustained stress check. Confirm insulation type and density from the project insulation specification before inputting into CAESAR II.

Key point

Insulation is not just a process requirement — it directly affects your stress analysis inputs (weight per metre) and support design (shoe height must accommodate insulation thickness). Always get the insulation specification before starting stress analysis.

Module 5 — Summary & Key Takeaways

These are the components you will specify and work with every day as a piping engineer.

  • From NPS 14 onwards, OD = NPS in inches. For NPS 12 and below, OD is always larger than the NPS number.
  • Long radius elbows (R = 1.5D) are the default — lower SIF, lower pressure drop. Short radius only where space genuinely prohibits long radius.
  • Eccentric reducer at pump suction: always Flat-Top (TOE) to prevent air pocket formation and pump cavitation.
  • One size drop at a branch: Reducing Tee. Two or more drops: Olet (Weldolet for butt-weld, Sockolet for socket-weld).
  • Weld Neck flanges for high-pressure/critical service. Slip-On for lower pressure non-critical. Blind for end closures and future tie-ins.
  • Flange pressure rating DECREASES with increasing temperature. Always check the B16.5 P-T table at your actual design temperature, not ambient.
  • Spiral wound gaskets (SS/graphite) for RF flanges in standard O&G service. RTJ for high-pressure pipeline and Class 900+ critical service.
  • A193 B7 / A194 2H is standard bolting. Sour service: B7M / 2HM (NACE MR0175). Cryogenic: A320 L7.

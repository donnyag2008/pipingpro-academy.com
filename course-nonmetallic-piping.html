Module 1

Non-Metallics in Oil & Gas

Before any pressure calculation, two questions decide a non-metallic line: what is it made of, and what kills it. This module places every non-metallic pipe in one family tree and shows where each belongs in an oilfield.

4 lessonsAPI 15LE · API 15S · SAES-L-610by Donny A. Bachtiar

What You Will Learn

  • Why non-metallic pipe is chosen over carbon steel in oilfield water and gas service
  • How to place any non-metallic pipe correctly within the materials family tree
  • The main oilfield non-metallic options and the duty each one suits
  • Why temperature and chemical compatibility — not corrosion — set the design limits

Section 1.1Why Leave Steel Behind

Carbon steel is strong and cheap, but in produced water, brine, water injection and firewater it corrodes — and the real expense is everything spent fighting that: internal corrosion allowance, coatings, cathodic protection, inhibitor injection, and a lifetime of inspection. Non-metallic pipe does not corrode electrochemically. There is no rust, no galvanic cell, no CP system, and no sacrificial wall thickness to specify.

It is also light, which cuts handling and transport, and the reinforced spoolable grades install in long continuous lengths with very few joints. The trade-off is honest: lower strength and a firm temperature ceiling. So the value case is strongest exactly where steel struggles — low-to-moderate pressure, ambient-to-warm, corrosive water and gas duty.

Field note

On the Fadhili offshore-gas increment study I ran, HDPE and reinforced composite pipe earned their place precisely on this logic — produced water and firewater lines where a corrosion-resistant, fast-to-install, electrically inert material removes a whole maintenance burden that steel would carry for thirty years.

Section 1.2The Family Tree

Every piping material sits somewhere on a single branching map. Metallic splits into ferrous and non-ferrous. Non-metallic splits into plastic and non-plastic (concrete, clay, glass — niche in oil and gas). Plastic then divides by how it responds to heat: thermoplastics can be re-melted and fused (HDPE, PVC, CPVC, PVDF, PP), while thermosets are cured once into a permanent shape and joined by bonding or lamination (GRE/GRP). This course lives in the plastic branch, with the oilfield grades highlighted.

The course covers the highlighted branch — plastic non-metallics

Section 1.3The Oilfield Toolbox

In practice the choice narrows to three options, each grounded in its own family of standards and suited to a different pressure–service window.

  • HDPE line pipe (PE100 / PE4710) — solid-wall thermoplastic; the workhorse for water, brine and low-pressure gas gathering. Sized by SDR, joined by butt or electrofusion, rated from long-term hydrostatic strength. Standards: API 15LE, AWWA M55, ASTM F2619; Saudi Aramco SAES-L-610, 01-SAMSS-051.
  • RTP / TCP — reinforced thermoplastic / composite spoolable pipe — a thermoplastic liner wrapped with fibre or steel-wire reinforcement and an outer jacket; spools onto a reel, installs fast over long runs, reaches far higher pressures. Examples: Flexstrong and SoluForce. Standards: API 15S, API 15HR.
  • GRE / GRP — glass-reinforced epoxy — rigid thermoset for firewater, seawater and produced water, especially where higher temperature or stiffness is needed than thermoplastics allow. Adhesive-bonded or laminated. Standards: ISO 14692, API 15HR.

Section 1.4What Actually Sets the Limits

This is the mental reset the rest of the course builds on. With steel you guard against wall thinning and pitting. With a non-metallic there is nothing to thin — degradation instead shows as softening, swelling, or embrittlement, and the usable envelope is fixed by two things working together.

The two governing limits

First, temperature: strength and pressure rating fall steeply as it rises, and most thermoplastics are capped around 60–82 °C for pressure service. Second, chemical compatibility, which is itself temperature- and concentration-dependent — a fluid that is harmless cold can attack the same pipe when hot. Get these two right and pressure, jointing and supports all follow.

That is why a non-metallic specification never starts with a wall-thickness sum. It starts with the service fluid and its temperature — and only then turns to rating and design.

Module 1 — Summary & Key Takeaways

Where non-metallics belong, and what governs them.

  • Non-metallics earn their place by removing corrosion cost, not by matching steel's strength.
  • Every non-metallic sits on one family tree; the oilfield grades are plastic — thermoplastic or thermoset.
  • The three practical options are HDPE line pipe, reinforced spoolable RTP/TCP, and rigid GRE/GRP.
  • Design is governed by temperature and chemical compatibility, evaluated together — not by wall loss.

Module 2

How Non-Metallics Carry Pressure

A steel line is rated from allowable stress. A polyethylene line is not — it is rated from how long it survives under stress. This module walks the HDPE design chain end to end, then lets you compute and derate a rating live.

5 lessonsAPI 15LE · PPI TR-3/4 · ISO 4427live calculator

What You Will Learn

  • Why HDPE is designed from long-term hydrostatic strength, not yield or tensile
  • The chain HDB → design factor → HDS
  • The API 15LE rating equation and the role of DR, FSF and TSF
  • How to compute and derate a PE4710 pressure rating yourself
  • The ASTM/API (HDB/HDS) versus ISO (MRS) design vocabularies

Section 2.1There Is No Allowable Stress

A thermoplastic creeps. Hold a steady load on HDPE and it keeps deforming, and eventually ruptures — sooner at higher stress, later at lower. Plotting that stress-versus-time-to-failure relationship and extrapolating it to a long life (typically 50 years) gives the Hydrostatic Design Basis (HDB): the categorised long-term hoop strength of the compound at a reference temperature, established by regression testing per ASTM D2837 and listed by the Plastics Pipe Institute.

So the starting number is not a yield point off a tensile test — it is a long-term strength read off a rupture curve. That single idea is the reason the rest of the method looks different from steel.

Section 2.2From HDB to HDS

The HDB is reduced by a design factor (DF) — a number below 1.0 that accounts for manufacturing variation, dimensional tolerance and other scatter — to give the Hydrostatic Design Stress (HDS), the stress the wall is actually designed to. For PE2708 and PE4710 the design factor is 0.63 at 73 °F. It is not a simple safety factor: because time-to-failure follows a power law in stress, a small drop in stress buys a large gain in life.

Design stress chain
HDS = HDB × DF
PE4710:   1600 psi × 0.63 = 1000 psi (6.9 MPa)
That 1000 psi is why it is called PE4710 — the "10" encodes HDS = 1000 psi at 73 °F, as "4" and "7" encode density and slow-crack-growth class.

Section 2.3The Rating Equation

The HDS is a stress; the pipe needs a pressure. The bridge is the dimension ratio (DR = OD ÷ minimum wall) — a thinner wall relative to diameter (higher DR) gives a lower rating.

API 15LE working-pressure rating
P = ( 2 · HDS · FSF · TSF ) ÷ ( DR − 1 )
P pressure rating · HDS at 73 °F · DR dimension ratio · FSF fluid service factor · TSF temperature service factor

Two of those terms are derating knobs set by service, both ≤ 1.0. The Fluid Service Factor (FSF) downrates for the transported medium — 1.0 for produced water, brine and dry gas, but 0.5 once the stream carries ≥ 2 % liquid hydrocarbons (crude, multiphase, wet gas). The Temperature Service Factor (TSF) downrates as the line runs hotter than the 73 °F reference, falling toward ~0.6 by 60 °C, above which the standard sends you to its elevated-temperature route.

Section 2.4Worked Example — One Pipe, Three Answers

f(x) PE4710 produced-water flowline, DR 11
Step 1 — design stress
HDB(73°F) = 1600 psi HDS = 1600 × 0.63 = 1000 psi (6.9 MPa)
Step 2 — base rating (produced water, ≤27°C)
FSF = 1.0, TSF = 1.0 P = 2 × 1000 × 1.0 × 1.0 / (11 − 1) P = 200 psi (≈ 13.8 bar)
Step 3 — warm it to ≈38°C → TSF 0.8
P = 2 × 1000 × 1.0 × 0.8 / 10 = 160 psi (≈ 11.0 bar)
Step 4 — switch to multiphase hydrocarbon → FSF 0.5
P = 2 × 1000 × 0.5 × 1.0 / 10 = 100 psi (≈ 6.9 bar) — half, just from the fluid

Sidebar — two vocabularies, one physics

ASTM / API (US): long-term strength is the HDB; design via HDB × DF = HDS, in psi. Grade code carries it (PE4710 → density 4, slow-crack 7, HDS 1000 psi). Used by API 15LE and most Aramco SAES references.   ISO 4427 (international): long-term strength is the MRS (minimum required strength) in MPa; design stress σ = MRS ÷ C, with C ≥ 1.25 for water; grades PE80 (MRS 8.0) and PE100 (MRS 10.0). Same rupture-curve physics, different label.

Build the habit

A PE rating is never a single number — it is a number tied to a DR, a fluid and a temperature. Quote any rating without those three and it is meaningless. Always state the basis alongside the value.

Module 2 — Summary & Key Takeaways

The HDPE design chain, end to end.

  • HDPE is rated from long-term hydrostatic strength (HDB), not from yield or tensile stress.
  • HDB × DF (0.63) = HDS, the working design stress; for PE4710 that is 1000 psi.
  • Pressure rating = 2·HDS·FSF·TSF ÷ (DR − 1) — geometry, fluid and temperature together.
  • FSF halves the rating for hydrocarbon service; TSF erodes it steadily with temperature.
  • ASTM/API speaks HDB/HDS in psi; ISO speaks MRS in MPa — same physics, different label.

Module 3

Reinforced & Composite Spoolable Pipe

When plain HDPE cannot reach the pressure, a thermoplastic liner is wrapped with reinforcement and a jacket. The result spools onto a reel, installs fast over long runs and carries far higher pressure — the RTP and TCP that increasingly replace small-bore steel flowlines.

4 lessonsAPI 15S · API 15HRRTP · TCP

What You Will Learn

  • Why reinforcement is added, and the three-layer construction of spoolable pipe
  • The difference between RTP, TCP and bonded vs unbonded systems
  • How the design basis shifts to qualified pressure and a burst safety factor (API 15S)
  • Real product envelopes — Flexstrong and SoluForce — and where each fits
  • The spoolable advantages that drive the steel-replacement business case

Section 3.1Why Reinforce — and How It's Built

Solid-wall HDPE is limited: to raise its pressure rating you must drop the DR (thicken the wall), which quickly becomes heavy, stiff and uneconomic. Reinforced pipe escapes that trap by letting a strong reinforcement layer carry the hoop load while a thermoplastic liner handles containment and corrosion resistance. The generic build is three layers:

  • Liner — a thermoplastic (commonly HDPE) that contains the fluid and resists corrosion and permeation.
  • Reinforcement — helically wound high-strength fibre (glass or aramid) or steel wire that carries the pressure load.
  • Outer jacket — a thermoplastic cover protecting the reinforcement from damage and the environment.

The two families differ in how the layers interact. RTP (Reinforced Thermoplastic Pipe) typically uses fibre reinforcement; TCP (Thermoplastic Composite Pipe) fully bonds a fibre-reinforced composite into a single fused wall. Steel-wire reinforced grades sit alongside these for the highest pressures. Bonded (fused) walls give the best fatigue and gas-tightness; unbonded (layered) walls are simpler and very flexible.

Section 3.2The Design Basis Shifts Again

Spoolable pipe is qualified and rated under API 15S (spoolable reinforced plastic line pipe) and API 15HR (high-pressure fibreglass). Rather than the HDPE DR equation of Module 2, the manufacturer establishes a long-term qualified pressure by regression testing of the composite, and the product is sold against a rated working pressure with a stated burst safety factor — commonly in the order of 3:1 — plus a temperature limit and a service (sour/CO₂) qualification.

What you specify

For spoolable pipe you generally select against the manufacturer's qualified envelope — rated pressure, maximum temperature, fluid/sour service and minimum bend radius (reel size) — rather than computing a wall thickness yourself. The diligence is in matching that qualified envelope to your service and confirming the qualification basis.

Section 3.4The Spoolable Advantage

The reel is the whole point. Long continuous lengths mean very few joints, which is where steel flowlines fail and slow down. The practical wins:

  • Speed — kilometres laid per day from a reel, with no field welding or NDE of girth welds.
  • Corrosion-free — no CP, no internal corrosion allowance, no inhibitor programme.
  • Steel replacement — the classic use case: re-lining or replacing corroded small-bore steel flowlines in produced-water, water-injection and gas-gathering duty.
  • Sour capability — the right grades carry sour service without the H₂S/CO₂ limits that constrain steel selection.
The constraint

Spoolable pipe trades pressure-temperature headroom and diameter for these gains. It dominates small-to-medium bore, low-to-moderate pressure, warm-not-hot duty — and gives way to steel or GRE as diameter, pressure or temperature climb.

Module 3 — Summary & Key Takeaways

Reinforcement buys pressure that solid-wall HDPE cannot reach.

  • A thermoplastic liner carries corrosion duty while reinforcement carries the hoop load.
  • RTP uses fibre; TCP fully bonds the composite; steel-wire grades reach the highest pressures.
  • Spoolable pipe is rated under API 15S / 15HR against a qualified pressure and burst safety factor.
  • You select against the manufacturer's qualified envelope, not a wall-thickness sum.
  • The business case is speed, corrosion-freedom and steel-flowline replacement in moderate duty.

Chemical Resistance & Material Selection

For a non-metallic, chemical compatibility is the real selection driver — and it is never a single yes/no. This module shows how to read a chemical resistance chart correctly, the failure modes that catch designers out, and a clean workflow from service to material.

4 lessonsPPI TR-19 · manufacturer chartsselection workflow

What You Will Learn

  • Why compatibility is temperature- and concentration-dependent, never a flat rating
  • How to read a resistance chart: resistant / conditional / not recommended / no data
  • The non-metallic failure modes — permeation, swelling, ESC, softening
  • How HDPE performs against key oilfield media (methanol, CO₂, H₂S, brine)
  • A repeatable selection workflow from service in to material out

Section 4.1Compatibility Is Not a Single Number

Published chemical resistance charts (the most comprehensive being PPI TR-19) list each fluid against each polymer at several temperatures — typically 20, 40 and 60 °C — precisely because a rating that is fine cold can fail hot. They also depend on concentration. A typical chart rates each combination on a four-level key:

Typical chemical-resistance rating key (after PPI / manufacturer charts)
SymbolMeaningDesign implication
+ResistantSuitable at that temperature/concentration
0Conditionally resistantLimited — reduced life, qualify carefully
−Not recommendedAvoid — expect attack
(blank)No dataUnknown — test or consult manufacturer
The hot-fluid trap

Always read the chart at your design temperature, not at ambient. A "+" at 20 °C that becomes "0" or "−" at 60 °C is the single most common way a non-metallic selection goes wrong.

Section 4.2How Non-Metallics Actually Fail Chemically

Unlike steel, chemical attack on a plastic does not thin the wall. It changes the material:

  • Permeation — small molecules (hydrocarbons, gases) diffuse through the wall; relevant to liners and to gas-tightness.
  • Swelling / softening — absorbed fluid plasticises the polymer, dropping strength and stiffness.
  • Environmental stress cracking (ESC) — combined stress and a specific chemical cause cracking below the normal strength; a classic polyolefin failure mode.
  • Embrittlement — oxidation or chemical change makes the material brittle over time.

This is why charts are explicitly guidelines only — most are developed from short (e.g. 48-hour) coupon immersion tests, and long-term, stressed, in-service behaviour can differ. Engineering judgement and, for critical duty, project-specific testing remain essential.

Section 4.3HDPE Against Oilfield Media

For the water and gas duty where HDPE is chosen, its resistance is broad. From published charts and field experience, PE is rated resistant to many key oilfield fluids:

  • Produced water, brine and salts — resistant; the core use case.
  • Methanol — resistant across concentrations; relevant to hydrate management.
  • Carbon dioxide (anhydrous) — resistant.
  • Hydrogen sulphide (aqueous and technically pure) — resistant; part of why non-metallics suit sour duty.
  • Hydrocarbons / crude — the weak point: permeation and swelling make plain HDPE unsuitable for hydrocarbon service, which is exactly why the FSF drops to 0.5 and why composite liners or different polymers are used.

Section 4.4A Selection Workflow

Put the two governing limits and the chart together into a repeatable sequence:

Service in → material out

1. Define the service fluid, concentration and design temperature. 2. Check chemical compatibility at that temperature on a reputable chart. 3. Confirm the temperature is within the material's pressure-service limit. 4. Establish the required pressure rating (HDPE method, or a qualified spoolable envelope). 5. Check secondary factors — permeation, ESC, UV, external loads. 6. For critical or borderline duty, confirm with the manufacturer and/or project testing.

Module 4 — Summary & Key Takeaways

Selecting on chemistry, the right way.

  • Chemical compatibility is temperature- and concentration-dependent — always read at design temperature.
  • Charts use a four-level key (resistant / conditional / not recommended / no data) and are guidelines only.
  • Non-metallics fail by permeation, swelling, ESC and embrittlement — not wall loss.
  • HDPE resists brine, methanol, CO₂ and H₂S well but is unsuitable for hydrocarbon service.
  • A disciplined service-to-material workflow prevents the common hot-fluid selection error.

Module 5

Design, Jointing & Installation

The properties that make non-metallics attractive — low stiffness, high expansion, viscoelastic creep — also make them behave unlike steel in the field. This final module covers the design consequences, how the pipe is joined, and the buried-line checks that keep it safe.

4 lessonsASME B31.3 Ch.VII · API 15LE Annex Cfusion · buckling

What You Will Learn

  • Viscoelasticity, creep and stress relaxation, and the 50-year design-life convention
  • Why low modulus and high thermal expansion dominate support and expansion design
  • The jointing methods — butt fusion, electrofusion, solvent cement, bonding, swage
  • External collapse / buckling of buried and submerged lines
  • The standards map and the installation considerations that matter

Section 5.1Viscoelasticity, Creep & the 50-Year Life

A thermoplastic is part-elastic, part-viscous. Under sustained load it deforms immediately (elastic) and then keeps deforming slowly over time (creep); under sustained strain its stress slowly decays (stress relaxation). The practical consequence is that the effective modulus depends on how long the load is applied — short-term values are far higher than long-term. This is exactly why design uses a long-term (50-year) basis, and why a single creep event such as a hung weight will keep sagging if not removed.

Upside of viscoelasticity

The same time-dependence gives polyethylene excellent resilience to short, sharp loads — water hammer and surge are absorbed far better than by rigid steel. Resistance and weakness come from the same property.

Section 5.2Low Modulus, High Expansion

Two numbers dominate the stress engineer's view of a plastic line. Its modulus of elasticity is roughly 1/300th of steel, and its coefficient of thermal expansion is far higher — for PE about 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ in/in/°F, several times that of steel.

  • Support spacing is governed by deflection (sag), not stress — aboveground plastic lines need far more frequent support than steel.
  • Thermal movement is large; an unrestrained line expands and contracts considerably with temperature, so the layout must accommodate or restrain it.
  • Thrust restraint is essential — the low modulus and high expansion mean joints must take longitudinal thrust, so fused, flanged or mechanically restrained joints are required (plain push-fit will pull out).

Section 5.3Jointing Methods

How a non-metallic is joined follows directly from whether it is thermoplastic or thermoset:

  • Butt fusion — square, heat and press two thermoplastic pipe ends; the primary HDPE field joint, full-pressure and homogeneous.
  • Electrofusion — a fitting with an embedded heating coil fuses the joint; ideal for tie-ins and repairs.
  • Solvent cement — for PVC/CPVC; the cement chemically welds the socket.
  • Adhesive bonding / lamination — for thermoset GRE (taper/taper or laminated); cannot be re-melted.
  • Swage & flanged end-fittings — for spoolable RTP/TCP, transitioning to standard flanges (often in 316SS, galvanised or carbon steel) for connection to the rest of the system.
Qualification & NDE

Fusion joints depend on operator technique, so jointer qualification and procedure control matter as much as the pipe itself — and conventional NDE is limited on fused plastic, so quality is built in through procedure and visual/destructive checks rather than radiography.

Section 5.4External Collapse, Codes & Installation

One failure mode unique to thin-wall non-metallics is external collapse — where external pressure (vacuum, submergence, or groundwater on an empty line) exceeds the pipe's buckling resistance. For an unconstrained round pipe this is governed by stiffness, not strength, and estimated from the modulus and DR (API 15LE Annex C gives the relation, with a safety factor of ~2 typical). Properly buried pipe is supported by the soil and far more resistant.

The governing standards bring it together: API 15LE and API 15S for the pipe, ASME B31.3 Chapter VII for nonmetallic in-plant piping, ASME B31.8 for gas service, Aramco SAES-L-610 for non-metallic in-plant piping, and AWWA M55 / the PPI Handbook for installation. Installation watch-items: UV protection during storage (carbon-black grades for long outdoor exposure), correct trench bedding, and fatigue/surge analysis where pumping causes recurring pressure cycles.

Module 5 — Summary & Key Takeaways

Behaving unlike steel — by design.

  • Viscoelastic creep and stress relaxation underpin the 50-year design-life basis.
  • Low modulus drives deflection-governed support spacing; high expansion drives thermal design.
  • Thrust-restraining joints (fusion, flange, mechanical) are mandatory — never plain push-fit.
  • External collapse of thin-wall pipe is a stiffness problem, worst on empty/submerged lines.
  • API 15LE/15S, ASME B31.3 Ch.VII and SAES-L-610 are the governing references.
